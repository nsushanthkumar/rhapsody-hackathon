This is the idea that Sushanth has pitched for a hackathon that we're thinking of running for Rhapsody. Rhapsody is Straive's (Gramener's) data analytics team internal annual fest, not particularly formal.

It includes fairly diverse teams and we want to excite them. Maybe they'll learn something, maybe we will learn something from the experiments that we might be able to run. In the past, we've had comic generation hackathons, storytelling hackathons, kinds of things that anyone can participate in. They don't necessarily need to have technical skills. Keeping this kind of an objective in mind and knowing my style of experimenting and the kind of work that I do, as well as Straive's' overall objective.

  • IDEATE prioritizing novelty over feasibility. Do not filter.
    • Choose 3–5 distinct people who'd see this differently. For each, generate 3 short candidate ideas.
    • Choose 2 UNRELATED domains and for each, extract 3 atomic structural rules, and use them to create 4 more candidate ideas.
    • List the 5 most obvious / conventional ideas across all candidates. Ban them, along with near-duplicates.
    • Merge all remaining ideas removing overlaps. Keep the set maximally diverse across mechanisms, users, and time horizons.
  • CONVERGE:
    • Critique each surviving idea: what assumption must hold, why it is non-obvious, and why it may fail.
    • Score each idea on impact, novelty, and speed.
    • Recommend the best practical idea(s) and the best wildcard idea(s), explaining the non-obvious insight each is built on.

Plan like an expert. In this context, first think about: - What patterns would an expert in this field check / recognize that beginners would miss? - What questions would an expert ask that a beginner would not know to? - What problems / failures would an expert anticipate that beginners may not be aware of? - What powerful & relevant mental models would an expert apply in this context?

Expert planning notes (before ideating)

A few things an expert running internal corporate creativity events would catch that a first-time organiser might miss.

Pattern recognition. "Cross-functional team" is necessary but not sufficient — without forced interdependence (rules that prohibit certain roles from doing certain things), engineers still drive and others "support." Sushanth's proposal mitigates this with judging rubrics, but rubrics are post-hoc; the failure happens upstream. Also: 80% of hackathon submissions are forgettable. Formats that guarantee memorable artefacts beat formats that hope for them.

Questions a beginner wouldn't ask. Is this event optimising for output or for experience? Rhapsody historically has been the latter (comic generation, storytelling — anyone can play). Sushanth's INIT(0→1) is squarely the former. Those are different events and shouldn't be conflated. Second: who gets excluded by the format? Overnight excludes parents and people with health constraints. "Build a prototype" excludes most non-engineers in spirit, even if not in letter. Third: what survives the event a year later? A trophy? A photo? A story? Designing backwards from the artefact that survives is more useful than designing the schedule.

Failure modes to anticipate.

  • Engineers carry; everyone else watches.
  • Output is unshowable to clients (so the marketing-asset ROI collapses).
  • The "0→1" filter is unenforceable — judges can't reliably distinguish genuine novelty from competent recombination in 24 hours.
  • 2 AM crash kills quality. Side quests help; they don't fix it.
  • Sales pain points produce B2B-flavoured ideas that don't match Rhapsody's celebratory mood.

Mental models worth applying.

  • Format as filter (the rules determine the population and the output more than the brief does).
  • Constraint multiplies creativity (sonnets, haikus, dogme95 — narrower brief, sharper output).
  • Power inversion, not power addition (real inclusion happens when the powerful are restricted, not when the weak are invited).
  • Generation is cheap, evaluation is scarce (AI inverted the value chain — Anand's own Bach Faucet / Jevons framing).
  • Tournament vs. festival (Sushanth's design is a tournament; past Rhapsody was a festival; choose deliberately).
  • Variance is the signal (with identical tools available to everyone, what diverges is the only interesting data).

With that lens, here's the ideation.

IDEATE

Five personas, three ideas each

A children's TV producer (Sesame Street / Bluey lens). Optimises for engagement, accessibility, learning through play.

  1. Show & Tell. Final demos are to actual children (employees' kids, 8–14). Kids vote. Any team whose pitch a 10-year-old can't explain back loses.
  2. Co-Pilot. Every team includes one non-employee child as a full member with equal vote on direction. Not decoration — forced naive perspective.
  3. The Explainer. Build a tool that teaches itself in 60 seconds — output is a self-onboarding artefact that makes a non-trivial Straive capability legible to a stranger.

A sports league commissioner (Adam Silver lens). Optimises for narrative, drama, repeat viewing. 4. Draft Format. Captains are chosen by lottery and draft teams live, on stage, in front of everyone — the drama is the event. Roles, skills, and past performance are public. 5. League Season. Not 24 hours — 24 days. Weekly micro-challenges, public leaderboard, mid-season trade window, finals weekend. 6. The Combine. Before any teaming, a public 4-hour combine tests individuals on micro-skills (prompt-writing, viz, narration, pitch). Public scores. Teams form afterwards using combine data.

A behavioural economist (Dan Ariely / Thaler lens). Optimises for revealing how people actually behave under constraint. 7. Reverse Hackathon. Start with an AI-generated baseline prototype of mediocre quality. Teams have 24 hours to break it — expose failure modes, find adversarial inputs, document fragility. Generation was free; evaluation is the prize. 8. Switch Day. At hour 12, every team's project is randomly handed to another team. Receiving team has 12 hours to finish, transform, or kill it. Final pitch is given by the second team. 9. Sunk Cost Challenge. All ideas published at hour 4. At hour 12 each team must publicly DOUBLE DOWN or KILL. Teams that kill spend remaining 12 hours trying to convince others to kill too. Meta-game about commitment vs. judgment.

A jazz musician / improv performer. Optimises for ensemble play, constraint-as-enabler. 10. Theme-and-Variations. One single prompt for the whole room. Twelve teams, twelve responses. The art is the divergence — same constraint, radically different answers. The exhibition of variance is the event. 11. Solo & Ensemble. Hour 0–4: each person builds something alone. Hour 4–12: random pairs combine solos. Hour 12–24: pairs of pairs combine into quads. Final output is composed, not authored. 12. Yes-And. Every team must build on top of something from a previous Rhapsody (the comic generator, the storytelling tools, an internal demo). Genuinely 1→2, honestly done — harder than 0→1 done badly.

A museum curator. Optimises for what artefact survives and how it gets told. 13. Future Exhibition 2031. Output isn't a prototype — it's a speculative artefact from five years from now. A fake product page, a fake client testimonial video, a fake academic paper. Final event is an exhibition visitors walk through. 14. Single Artefact. The deliverable is one thing: one image, one PDF, one tweet, one URL, one 30-second video. Teams compete on what single artefact best represents their idea if it's all that survives. 15. Time Capsule. Build something that unlocks in 1 year. Must improve with age, be relevant 12 months later, and contain a "do not open until" mechanism.

Two unrelated domains, atomic rules, more ideas

Domain A — Iron Chef / Bake Off cooking shows.

  • A1. Secret ingredient revealed at T=0; no preparation allowed.
  • A2. Multiple rounds with different formats — speed, signature, pressure test.
  • A3. Judges consume the output and narrate the experience live; subjective, embodied evaluation.
  1. Iron Hacker. Two teams at a time, on stage, live. Secret "ingredient" (a dataset, an API, a constraint) revealed at start. Audience watches them build for 60 minutes. Judges use the output in real time and react out loud. Eliminates the "tell vs. show" gap.
  2. Pressure Test Rotation. The 24 hours split into three 8-hour rounds — Speed (build in 2 hours), Signature (showcase your team's strongest skill), Constraint (one arm tied: no code / no internet / only AI-generated assets).
  3. Showstopper. Final outputs revealed simultaneously to the whole room on synchronised screens — 90 seconds of narration, then silent vote. Borrowed directly from Bake Off's reveal moment.
  4. Taste Test. The entire evaluation is consumption-based. Judges don't read decks. They use each prototype for 5 minutes while narrating the experience to the room, like food critics on camera.

Domain B — Tabletop RPG (D&D).

  • B1. Character classes have asymmetric abilities — each role has unique powers and prohibitions.
  • B2. Dice rolls introduce shared, witnessed randomness mid-play.
  • B3. A Game Master controls the world, introducing obstacles and rewards in response to player actions.
  1. Class System. Five fictional classes — Architect, Wizard, Bard, Strategist, Oracle. Each has POWERS (only this class can do X) and PROHIBITIONS (this class cannot do Y). E.g., Wizards (engineers) cannot speak to judges — only the Bard (marketing) can. Architects cannot type — only diagram. Real interdependence by design.
  2. GM-Driven Hackathon. Every 4 hours, a Game Master (Vijayam, or a panel) drops a plot twist. "A client just asked for a legal pivot." "Your data source is down — here's a 1000x messier backup." "A competitor just announced your idea." Teams must respond. Reality changes mid-project — bake it in.
  3. Roll for Initiative. At each checkpoint teams roll dice. The roll determines what resource they get next — extra time, an expert consult, API credits, 30 minutes of a senior's attention. Randomness creates story.
  4. The Campaign. Multi-session arc. Teams have ongoing "characters" and projects. Rhapsody is Session 1 of a longer quarterly campaign with evolving lore.

Banning the obvious

Five ideas (and near-duplicates) are out:

  • Standard "build a working AI prototype in 24 hours" (the canonical hackathon — Sushanth's proposal already covers this lane).
  • Generic "use this dataset to build a dashboard" data-hack.
  • "Build a chatbot / LLM wrapper" (the obvious AI hackathon shape).
  • Comic / storytelling generation (already done in past Rhapsodies).
  • Pure ideation + pitch with no build (the safe, low-energy alternative).

Anything that's a structural clone of Sushanth's INIT(0→1) (cross-functional team builds a prototype from a sales pain point) is also off the table — the point is alternatives, not minor variants.

CONVERGE

Critique, scored

Scores are out of 5. Impact = how memorable + commercially useful the output. Novelty = how genuinely distinct from a standard hackathon. Speed = ease of executing this Rhapsody (logistics, learning curve, risk).

1. Iron Hacker. Assumption: people perform creatively under live audience pressure; judges can use a tool meaningfully in 5 minutes. Non-obvious insight: evaluation by consumption eliminates the demo-vs-reality gap that makes most hackathon scoring noisy. Failure mode: engineers freeze on stage; throughput too low for 12+ teams. Impact 4 · Novelty 4 · Speed 2.

2. Class System. Assumption: people accept hard role prohibitions without cheating, and the prohibitions don't feel like cosplay. Non-obvious insight: cross-functional collaboration is enforced by restricting the powerful, not by inviting the powerless. Inclusion = inversion. Failure mode: feels gimmicky; engineers feel hobbled; classes map awkwardly to messy real skills. Impact 5 · Novelty 5 · Speed 4.

3. Reverse Hackathon. Assumption: adversarial thinking is generative; "breaking" is celebrated, not seen as negative. Non-obvious insight: in an AI-saturated world, generation is cheap and evaluation is the scarce capability — this format trains the actually scarce skill. Strongly resonant with Verifiable Autonomy and LLM-Psychologist framing. Failure mode: harder to demo to clients ("we broke things" sells worse than "we built things"); risk of feeling pessimistic. Best practical pick: Theme-and-Variations.

The non-obvious insight is that in 2026, everyone in the room has the same AI tools. The only interesting signal left is divergence. One prompt to all twelve teams. Twelve answers walking through the same constraint, ending up in radically different places. The output is a wall of twelve responses you can literally line up and look at — comparison is the event. Every team produces something, nothing is wasted, and the meta-finding (what made teams diverge?) is exactly the kind of LLM-Psychology / human-as-interface observation Straive can use externally. Best wildcard: Class System.

The non-obvious insight: cross-functional collaboration fails not because non-tech people aren't invited but because tech people aren't prohibited. Real interdependence requires power inversion. Second wildcard: Reverse Hackathon.

This is the one that's most "you," Anand.   2. Run a festival format (Theme-and-Variations is the lead candidate) — keeping the historic Rhapsody DNA.

Not the best engineer, not the most nervous marketer — the median. Three options:

  • Structure the team — mandate composition (1 of each role) and hope it works.
  • Restrict the team — give each role hard prohibitions ("engineers cannot present"; "marketers cannot touch code"). Power inversion, not just power addition.

How Vijayam's GCC theme fits into Twelve Answers

There's a tension to resolve up front. Anand-style Twelve Answers is one prompt, twelve interpretations — variance is the artefact. Vijayam-style is one theme (GCCs), teams pick their own function/industry — focus is the artefact. These look different but they collapse into one design if the prompt is invariant in form but variable in domain. That is: a single prompt all teams answer, but the prompt's structure forces each team to pick a GCC function (finance, HR, supply chain, R&D, customer ops, procurement, legal) and/or a GCC parent industry (banking, pharma, consumer, tech). The prompt is one; the answers diverge because each team chooses a different slice of the GCC universe.

The downstream "GCC summit" plan Vijayam mentions is what makes this commercially serious. The hackathon stops being internal entertainment — it's the content pipeline for a Straive GCC showcase event. Twelve working demos walk into a room of GCC heads. That changes what "good" looks like for the prompt: it has to produce things you'd be proud to show a real GCC CFO or a Goldman Hyderabad MD.

His suggestion of a pre-event recorded GCC primer, posted a day or two before, is also a quietly excellent piece of design. It levels the room for non-GCC-savvy people (designers, junior consultants, marketers) without watering down the theme. The primer also becomes a reusable Straive asset in its own right — a 20-minute "GCC landscape in 2026" explainer that can be republished externally with light editing. Free leverage.

Specific prompt candidates

Five strong options. Each is GCC-themed, format-agnostic (images, video, audio, app, report, game, agent all qualify), 8-hour-buildable with AI tools, and forces a domain choice that produces variance.

A. "Build what a GCC's first AI-native hire would build on day one."

The strongest balance of variance and commercial bite. The persona is specific (first AI-native hire), the timeframe is constrained (day one — so it must be small and shippable), and the function/industry is entirely up to the team. One team builds a procurement copilot for a pharma GCC; another builds a compliance triage agent for a banking GCC; another builds a "first 30 days" onboarding tool for a tech GCC. Twelve different "day one builds" across twelve different GCC slices. Highly demoable at the downstream GCC summit — every output is a thing the audience could plausibly deploy.

B. "Build the thing a GCC would quietly steal from its parent company."

Inversion-shaped. The whole GCC narrative in 2026 is we're not a cost center, we're a value center — this prompt makes that the brief. Teams have to identify something a Hyderabad office now does better than its New York / Zurich / London parent and build the artefact that proves it. Outputs lean toward benchmarking tools, productivity demonstrators, internal-marketing artefacts, or speculative product extensions. Most Anand-shaped of the five — provocative, counter-narrative, very publishable as a study. Slightly less directly commercial than (A); the GCC head you demo it to needs a moment to register the framing.

C. "Build the artefact that justifies a GCC's existence in 2031."

Future-shifted. Five years out, when AI has eaten half of what GCCs currently do, what's left? Teams build the speculative artefact — a 2031 internal pitch deck, a 2031 client testimonial, a 2031 GCC org chart, a 2031 service catalogue — that answers the existential question. Highly aesthetic, very designer-friendly, beautifully publishable. Risk: a GCC head at the summit may find it too speculative to act on immediately.

D. "Build the demo that closes a GCC client in the next meeting."

The most Vijayam-shaped prompt. Direct, commercial, every output is by definition a pitch asset for the downstream summit. Teams pick a GCC function and build the live demo that would actually win the room. Output is unambiguously useful to Sales. Risk: the prompt is too convergent — most teams will end up building variations of the same three or four obvious things, and the variance story collapses.

E. "Build the thing a GCC head would expense without asking their CFO."

Friction-shaped. The implicit constraint is small enough to slip through, useful enough to justify itself. Forces teams to build precise, scoped tools rather than ambitious platforms. Function/industry choice is wide open. Outputs are concrete, demoable, and fit the GCC summit narrative ("here are twelve things you could deploy this quarter without going through procurement"). Slightly less narratively rich than (A) or (B) but the highest realism-to-effort ratio.

Three reasons.

It satisfies both you and Vijayam. You get variance — twelve teams, twelve functions, twelve industries, twelve very different "day one" builds. Vijayam gets focus — every output is a deployable thing for a real GCC, not a speculative artefact. The synthesis is genuine, not a compromise.
