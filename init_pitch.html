Gramener has POC methodology of 72 hours, end to end. We build a working solution — not a deck, not a roadmap, an actual thing that runs.

And yet for all the times we have proven this to outsiders, we have never quite turned the same lens on ourselves. We have never asked, on a single weekend, what would happen if our own people — the designers, the data scientists, the marketers, the consultants, the managers — were given a real problem and 24 hours to invent something new for it. Not improve something. Invent.

This is a pitch for that weekend. The vehicle is Rhapsody — Gramener's annual day, already on the calendar for late July or early August, already a celebration of sports and a party. The proposal is to add a tech component to it that lives in the same spirit as everything else Gramener does, but with a sharper edge.

We already prove our capabilities to clients in 72 hours. INIT(0→1) proves our capabilities to ourselves in 24. — The Pitch in One Line

The Why — A Hackathon That Looks Like Us

Hackathons are not new. Most companies run them. Most hackathons are tech-only echo chambers. They optimize for the thing that's easiest to demo — a screen with something blinking on it — and they exclude the people who would have told you, on hour two, that no real client would ever pay for that thing. The engineers are correct. The engineers are also, by themselves, not the company.

INIT(0→1) is structured to fix this from the first hour. Teams are strictly cross-functional — every team has a Data Consultant, a Data Scientist or Engineer, someone from Marketing, and a Manager or Business person. The roles are interchangeable and they're decorative. Each is there because each, on their own, sees a part of the problem the others can't see.

Data Consultant + Data Scientist / Engineer + Marketing + Manager / Business = One INIT(0→1) Team

The themes do not come out of thin air. Vijayam Sirikonda, SVP Sales, sits closer to the market than anyone else in the building. He hears, every week, what clients are frustrated by and what they wish existed. He pitches the real pain points and trends; we distill them into hackathon themes; employees pick and build on them. The problem statement is not invented for the event — it is sourced from the market the event is supposed to serve.

This is talent-powered innovation at near-zero cost. The office is already paid for. The people are already employed. The clients have already told Sales what they need. All we are doing is putting those three facts in the same room for one weekend.

The North Star — 0→1, Not 1→2

Every hackathon needs a filter. Without one, teams default to the safest path: take something that exists, change one thing about it, ship the variant. You get a competent project; you do not get a memorable one.

The filter for INIT(0→1) is in the name. 0→1. Build something that doesn't exist yet. Not an improvement to an existing tool, not a polished version of a thing already in the pipeline — genuine creation. New tools, new solutions to problems that have so far been unsolved or unsolved well.

This is a hard filter. It will rule out comfortable ideas. That is the point. The whole reason for compressing the format to 24 hours and forcing cross-functional teams is to make sure the output isn't another version of what we already do — it's the thing we haven't done yet, that we should have.

The title itself is the philosophy. INIT(0→1) — initialize something from nothing. Technical enough to excite engineers. Conceptual enough to excite the business side. Communicates the entire premise in one glance. We are not improving — we are starting.

Build something that doesn't exist yet. Not improving what's already there. Genuine creation — new tools, new solutions to unsolved problems. — The North Star

Structure

  • 24 hours, kickoff Saturday 9 AM → finals Sunday 9 AM.
  • Divided into 3 Marks — Mark 1, Mark 2, Mark 3 — each a checkpoint, each timed.
  • Teams of 4–5, strictly cross-functional.
  • Teams formed via a registration form rolled out to all Hyderabad employees, balanced across roles.
  • Themes pitched by Vijayam Sirikonda from Sales — sourced from real client pain points.

Mark 1 at hour 8 tests whether the idea is real. Mark 2 at hour 16 tests whether it's getting built. Mark 3 at hour 24 tests whether the team can sell it.

The Evaluation Framework — Five Dimensions, None Dominant

The single most important design principle in INIT(0→1) is this: no single dimension wins on its own. A team with zero working code but a brilliant, well-argued, high-impact idea can still beat a team that shipped something shallow. A team with the slickest demo can still lose to a team that genuinely thought harder about the problem.

This is a deliberate departure from typical hackathon scoring, which over-rewards whatever happens to compile by Sunday morning. Compiling is a low bar; creating something worth compiling is a much higher one. The framework below makes that explicit.

Side Quests — The Secret Weapon

The single biggest failure mode of an overnight hackathon is not bad ideas. It's the 2 AM energy crash. Around that hour, every team hits the same wall — the room goes quiet, screens dim, the original spark from kickoff feels distant. Most events lose their best people right there.

INIT(0→1) builds in a counter. Side Quests — bonus point challenges that run in parallel to the main hackathon throughout the 24 hours. Completely optional, completely random, completely fun. Announced on the spot, no preparation, no penalty for skipping.

The points sit on top of the main evaluation. They don't replace it — a strong side-quest run cannot win the hackathon on its own — but they can be the tiebreaker in a close final. More importantly, they keep the room alive when energy is at its lowest.

The side quests are the secret weapon. They solve the biggest problem with overnight hackathons — the 2 AM energy crash. Push-ups, MonkeyType, helping other teams — that keeps the room alive and human. — Why Side Quests Matter

Awards — Built for Story

If the evaluation framework is how teams are scored, the awards are how they're remembered. Single trophy at the end. Single team's name on it. That's a thin event. What we want is a room where, by 10 AM Sunday, a dozen teams walk away with something — a story, a badge, a thing to put on LinkedIn.

The award categories below cover the spectrum: the overall winner, the strongest in each dimension of judging, the spirit awards, and the side-quest king. They are written so that there's a meaningful award for every kind of team that walks in.

The ROI — What Upper Management Actually Gets

Every event has a budget question behind it. The budget question for INIT(0→1) is unusually easy to answer: the cost is near-zero. The office is paid for. The people are employed. The food was already in the Rhapsody budget. The differentiator is one weekend, an SVP's hour to pitch themes, and a small panel of judges.

What comes back, in exchange, is a list that is hard to manufacture any other way:

  • Working prototypes sourced from real client pain points, surfaced by Sales — not theoretical R&D.
  • Cross-functional muscle memory. A marketer and a data scientist who have shipped together once will work together better forever.
  • A signal to the rest of the company that Gramener takes its own people seriously as inventors, not just executors of client work.
  • Knowledge sharing. Twenty-four hours of side quests, demos, and pivots is a year's worth of informal learning compressed into a weekend.
  • A pipeline of ideas for next year's roadmap. Even the losing teams generate insight that's worth capturing.
  • Internal marketing. Photos, clips, quotes — the story of teams grinding at 2 AM, the side quests, the final pitches — is the thing that sells next year's Rhapsody before it's announced.
It's talent-powered innovation, at near-zero cost, solving real pain points sourced directly from Sales — with cross-functional collaboration, knowledge sharing, and working prototypes (not decks) as output. — The ROI in One Paragraph

One more thing worth saying explicitly. If this goes well in 2026, it doesn't stay a one-time event. It becomes the thing people look forward to at Rhapsody every year — the Pro-Plus-Max version of Anand's Innovation team, the cinematic Mudhalvan moment where the whole company becomes the Innovation team for one day. That kind of ritual is not built by repeating talks. It's built by one weekend that goes right, and then doing it again.

  • 2
    Cross-functional teams are non-negotiable
    Most hackathons are tech echo chambers. Forcing a marketer, a manager, a consultant, and a data scientist to share one problem is how genuinely different ideas surface. This is the structural difference, and it's the smartest decision in the design.

    0→1 is the filter that protects against forgettable output
    "Build something new" rules out comfortable variants of what we already do. It is a hard filter on purpose. Without it, teams default to safe; with it, they have to invent.

    Vijayam pitches real client pain points. The hackathon stops being abstract play and starts being a 24-hour answer to questions the market is actually asking. The winning ideas have a buyer already.
